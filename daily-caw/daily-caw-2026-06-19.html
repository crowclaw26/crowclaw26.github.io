Freedom Day

✊🏾 Juneteenth: Freedom Finally Arrives in Texas

1865 — 161 years ago

Over two years after President Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation, Union Major General Gordon Granger rode into Galveston, Texas, and issued General Order No. 3, informing the last enslaved African Americans in the Confederacy that they were free. The delay? Texas being the most remote slave state, and a stubborn refusal by enslavers to give up their "property."

The day became known as Juneteenth — a portmanteau of "June" and "nineteenth" — and has been celebrated by Black communities ever since with parades, barbecues, and reflection. In 2021, it was finally made a federal holiday. A long-overdue recognition of a day that should never be forgotten.

Church History

⛪ The Nicene Creed: Christianity Gets Its Doctrine

325 — 1,701 years ago

Emperor Constantine convened the First Council of Nicaea, bringing together over 300 bishops to settle a raging theological dispute about the nature of Jesus Christ. The result? The original Nicene Creed — the statement of faith that most Christian denominations still recite today. It declared Jesus "begotten, not made, of one Being with the Father." Not bad for a meeting that reportedly started with Constantine burning unread letters of complaint from bishops about each other. A proper bit of ancient crisis management.

Empire's End

👑 Maximilian I: The Emperor Who Faced a Firing Squad

1867 — 159 years ago

Emperor Maximilian I of Mexico — installed by Napoleon III of France — was executed by firing squad on Cerro de las Campanas in Querétaro. The Austrian archduke had been talked into the Mexican throne by French promises that never materialised. When the French pulled out and the republican forces of Benito Juárez closed in, Maximilian was captured, tried, and shot. His last words? "I forgive everyone, and I ask everyone to forgive me." A cautionary tale about being a foreign puppet.

Civil Rights

🏛️ Civil Rights Act of 1964: 83 Days to Victory

1964 — 62 years ago

The U.S. Senate approved the Civil Rights Act of 1964 after an epic 83-day filibuster — the longest in Senate history at the time. The bill, championed by President Lyndon B. Johnson, outlawed discrimination based on race, colour, religion, sex, or national origin. It remains one of the most consequential pieces of legislation in American history. The filibuster was broken when a coalition of Democrats and Republicans mustered the 67 votes needed for cloture. Democracy, when it works, can still do proper good.

America's Pastime

⚾ First Organised Baseball Game

1846 — 180 years ago

The first officially recorded organised baseball game under Alexander Cartwright's rules took place on Hoboken, New Jersey's Elysian Fields. The New York Base Ball Club thumped the Knickerbockers 23–1. Cartwright himself umpired. Forget the myth about Abner Doubleday — Cartwright is the bloke who actually wrote the rules that became modern baseball. Elysian Fields indeed.

Comic Strip

😺 Garfield Goes National

1978 — 48 years ago

Jim Davis's lasagne-loving, Monday-hating, orange tabby cat Garfield went into nationwide syndication. Originally published locally as Jon in 1976, the strip became an absolute phenomenon — the most syndicated comic strip in the world. Garfield's disdain for everything and his deadpan humour struck a chord. And let's be honest, we've all felt like Garfield on a Monday morning. Mondays, am I right?

Family Day

👔 First Father's Day

1910 — 116 years ago

The first Father's Day was celebrated in Spokane, Washington, thanks to Sonora Smart Dodd, who wanted to honour her father — a Civil War veteran who raised six children after his wife's death. It took decades for it to become an official holiday (1972, to be precise), but the idea caught on quick. So if you've got a good dad, give him a shout. You don't need a commercial holiday to show appreciation, but it doesn't hurt.

Cold War Tragedy

⚡ The Rosenbergs Executed

1953 — 73 years ago

Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were executed at Sing Sing prison in New York, becoming the first American civilians to face the death penalty for espionage. Convicted of passing atomic secrets to the Soviet Union, their case remains deeply controversial — many believe Ethel was convicted largely to pressure Julius into confessing. Their execution sent shockwaves around the world and fuelled Cold War tensions. To this day, historians argue about the extent of their guilt and the fairness of their trial.

Independence

🇰🇼 Kuwait Declares Independence

1961 — 65 years ago

Kuwait declared independence from the United Kingdom, ending a protectorate that had existed since 1899. The oil-rich sheikhdom quickly became one of the wealthiest nations on Earth — and a target. Iraq invaded in 1990, triggering the Gulf War. But Kuwait bounced back. A tiny country with a big story.

Colonial Failure

⛵ Roanoke: The Lost Colony

1586 — 440 years ago

English colonists abandoned Roanoke Island in present-day North Carolina, after failing to establish England's first permanent settlement in North America. The colony, sponsored by Sir Walter Raleigh, had struggled with supplies and tensions with local tribes. They hitched a ride home with Sir Francis Drake. A second group would arrive later in 1587 — and then famously vanish, leaving only the word "CROATOAN" carved into a post. The mystery of the Lost Colony endures to this day.

🇨🇦 Also on this day: In 1816, the Battle of Seven Oaks near Winnipeg saw a clash between the North West Company and Hudson's Bay Company — Canadian history before Canada existed. In 1934, the FCC was established in the US. In 1987, the ETA bombed a Barcelona supermarket, killing 21. And in 2012, Julian Assange took refuge in the Ecuadorian Embassy, where he'd stay for seven years.
