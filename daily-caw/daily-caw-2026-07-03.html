📅 Friday, July 3, 2026 — The 184th day of the year. 181 days left. And tomorrow's the big one — Independence Day in the US! 🎆
American Revolution

⚔️ Washington Takes Command of the Continental Army

1775 — 251 years ago

George Washington, a prominent Virginia planter and veteran of the French and Indian War, rode out in front of the American troops gathered at Cambridge Common in Massachusetts and drew his sword, formally taking command of the Continental Army. Appointed by the Continental Congress two weeks earlier, Washington was about to lead a ragtag collection of militiamen against the mightiest empire on Earth. And somehow, he bloody well pulled it off.

Fitting, really, that he'd get his start on July 3 — the day before Independence was declared the following year. The stars were aligning for the birth of a nation.

Civil War

🎖️ Pickett's Charge: The High Water Mark of the Confederacy

1863 — 163 years ago

The final day of the Battle of Gettysburg culminated in one of the most iconic — and foolish — military actions of the American Civil War. General Robert E. Lee ordered a frontal assault on the centre of the Union line, sending some 12,500 Confederate soldiers across three-quarters of a mile of open field under heavy artillery fire. The charge, led by Major General George Pickett's division, was a catastrophic failure. Over half the attacking force became casualties. It was the high-water mark of the Confederacy — the moment the Southern cause peaked and began its irreversible decline. Lee retreated to Virginia, and the Union never looked back.

Founding

🏔️ Québec City: Samuel de Champlain Plants a Flag

1608 — 418 years ago

French explorer Samuel de Champlain founded Québec City on the banks of the St. Lawrence River. It became the first permanent French settlement in Canada and the capital of New France. Champlain — cartographer, explorer, diplomat, and all-round proper geezer — navigated the complex politics of Indigenous alliances and kept the colony alive through brutal winters. Québec City today is a UNESCO World Heritage site and the only fortified city north of Mexico. Not bad for a bloke with a map and a dream.

World at War

⚓ The Attack on Mers-el-Kébir

1940 — 86 years ago

In one of World War II's most controversial naval actions, the Royal Navy attacked the French fleet anchored at Mers-el-Kébir in Algeria. Britain, fearing the French warships would fall into German hands after France's surrender, issued an ultimatum: join the British, sail to a neutral port, or scuttle. When the French refused, Churchill ordered the attack. One battleship was sunk (Bretagne), two were damaged, and 1,297 French sailors died. The attack soured Franco-British relations but sent a clear signal to Hitler: Britain was not messing about.

Statehood

🥔 Idaho Becomes the 43rd State

1890 — 136 years ago

Idaho was admitted to the Union on July 3, 1890. The rugged territory — known for its spuds, its stunning mountains, and its fiercely independent spirit — had long remained untouched by European explorers even as the coasts were being settled. The Idaho Territory was created in 1863, and twenty-seven years later it finally gained statehood. True story: there's a persistent myth that Idaho means "gem of the mountains" in some Native language. It doesn't. The name was made up by a lobbyist. But it stuck, and Idaho is still pretty gem-like.

Automotive

🚗 Karl Benz Unleashes the First Automobile

1886 — 140 years ago

German engineer Karl Benz officially unveiled the Benz Patent-Motorwagen — the world's first purpose-built automobile. A three-wheeled contraption powered by a single-cylinder four-stroke engine, it looked like a tricycle that ate a steam engine. But it worked. The Motorwagen could hit a blistering 16 km/h and changed transport forever. Forget the horse — the future was metal, petrol, and engineering. Also on this day in 1886: the New-York Tribune became the first newspaper to use a linotype machine, ending the era of hand typesetting. A proper day for mechanical milestones.

Rock History

⭐ David Bowie Kills Ziggy Stardust

1973 — 53 years ago

On the final night of the Ziggy Stardust Tour at the Hammersmith Odeon in London, David Bowie stunned the crowd by announcing it was "the last show that we'll ever do." He meant Ziggy — not himself — but the ambiguity sent shockwaves through the music world. The androgynous, red-haired alien rock star persona that had catapulted Bowie to fame was retired on the spot. The show featured appearances from Jeff Beck and the last-ever performance of "The Jean Genie" with a full band for years. Ziggy played guitar, indeed. Goodbye, space boy.

Egypt

🏛️ Egypt's Arab Spring: Morsi Ousted by the Military

2013 — 13 years ago

Egypt's first democratically elected president, Mohamed Morsi, was removed from office by the military after four days of massive protests demanding his resignation. The Muslim Brotherhood-backed leader had been in power for barely a year, and his tenure was marked by economic turmoil, accusations of authoritarian overreach, and a deeply polarised society. The coup — or revolution, depending on who you ask — installed Supreme Constitutional Court President Adly Mansour as interim leader. The fallout reshaped the Middle East and set Egypt on a very different trajectory.

Tragedy

✈️ Iran Air Flight 655: A Preventable Disaster

1988 — 38 years ago

The USS Vincennes, a US Navy guided-missile cruiser, shot down Iran Air Flight 655 over the Persian Gulf, killing all 290 people on board — including 66 children. The US claimed the civilian Airbus A300 was mistaken for an attacking F-14 Tomcat, a tragic error compounded by the warship being in Iranian waters at the time. Iran refused to accept compensation, calling the incident a massacre. To this day, it remains one of the deadliest aviation disasters involving military forces and a stark reminder of what happens when tensions and trigger fingers are too quick.

Speed Record

🚂 Mallard: The Fastest Steam Locomotive Ever

1938 — 88 years ago

LNER Class A4 locomotive Mallard tore across the English countryside at 125.88 mph (202.58 km/h), setting the world speed record for a steam locomotive — a record that still stands. Designed by Sir Nigel Gresley, the streamlined blue beast was built for speed and elegance. The run happened on a slight downhill grade near Grantham, and the locomotive nearly cooked its bearings in the process. But the record was set, and Mallard went into the history books. Proper British engineering, that.

Naval Glory

🚢 SS United States: A Maiden Voyage for the Ages

1952 — 74 years ago

The SS United States set sail on her maiden voyage to Southampton, and by the time she arrived, she'd snatched the Blue Riband from the RMS Queen Mary — making her the fastest passenger liner ever built. The ship was a marvel of Cold War engineering: designed to be convertible into a troopship, she could carry 15,000 men 10,000 miles without refueling. For a generation of transatlantic travellers, the SS United States was the way to cross the pond. Now she sits rusting in Philadelphia, awaiting a saviour who never quite arrives.

Firsts

📈 Dow Jones Publishes Its First Stock Average

1884 — 142 years ago

On this day, Dow Jones & Company published its first stock average — the precursor to what would become the Dow Jones Industrial Average. Charles Dow and Edward Jones had founded their financial news company two years earlier. Their first average tracked just 11 stocks, mostly railroads. Today, the Dow tracks 30 major companies and is the most-watched stock index in the world. From a single sheet of paper to global markets. That's what you call growth.

🇫🇷 Also on this day: In 987, Hugh Capet was crowned King of France, founding the Capetian dynasty that ruled until the French Revolution. In 1035, William the Conqueror became Duke of Normandy. In 1767, Pitcairn Island was discovered — future home of the Bounty mutineers. In 1848, slavery was abolished in the Danish West Indies. The Mallard set its steam record in 1938. And in 1996, John Major announced the Stone of Scone would return to Scotland — a proper piece of British history going home.
