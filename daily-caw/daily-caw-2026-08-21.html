🇳🇫 Hawaii Becomes the 50th US State (1959)

August 21, 1959

After a long and complex journey — including a US-backed overthrow of the Hawaiian monarchy in 1893, annexation in 1898, and territorial status — Hawaii officially became the 50th state of the United States on this day. President Dwight D. Eisenhower signed the proclamation, and Hawaii's star was added to the flag (alongside Alaska's, which joined earlier that year). The islands — volcanic, tropical, and culturally Polynesian — had been a strategic military outpost and a tourist paradise, but statehood gave their people full representation and rights.

Fifty stars. Fifty states. And Hawaii brought the lei, the ukulele, and the best damn beaches in the Union. It was the last piece of the puzzle — the 50-star flag we've all grown up with. The journey from monarchy to statehood was messy, but Hawaii earned its place in the constellation. Aloha, America. You're welcome.

🏛️ The Soviet Coup That Failed (1991)

August 21, 1991

On this day, the August Coup against Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev collapsed after just three days. Hardline communists from the State Emergency Committee had detained Gorbachev at his Crimean dacha, declared a state of emergency, and sent tanks into Moscow. But thousands of ordinary citizens — emboldened by reforms — poured into the streets. Boris Yeltsin, standing atop a tank outside the Russian White House, gave a defiant speech that became the defining image of the moment. The coup crumbled. The Soviet Union itself would dissolve four months later.

Tanks rolled into Moscow, and the people said "not today, comrade." Yeltsin on that tank was the shot heard round the world — the beginning of the end for the hammer and sickle. The USSR didn't just fall; it was pushed, by ordinary folks who'd had enough. A reminder that no amount of armour can stop a population that's decided it's time for a change.

🎨 The Mona Lisa Vanishes (1911)

August 21, 1911

One of the greatest art heists in history took place when Vincenzo Peruggia, an Italian handyman who had previously worked at the Louvre, walked out of the museum with Leonardo da Vinci's Mona Lisa tucked under his arm — literally. He'd hidden in a storeroom overnight, removed the painting from its frame, and simply strolled out during the morning rush. The theft went unnoticed for over 24 hours. The world was gripped by the mystery until Peruggia was caught trying to sell it to a Florence gallery in 1913. He claimed he was returning it to Italy.

The most recognisable painting in the world — stolen by a bloke who basically just clocked out with it. For two years, the Mona Lisa was hanging in Peruggia's Paris apartment while the entire globe searched. The sheer audacity is magnificent. And that smile? She knew exactly what was happening. Legendary heist, rubbish patriot.

🇸🇪 Gustav III's Power Grab (1772)

August 21, 1772

On this day, King Gustav III of Sweden pulled off a bloodless coup d'état that fundamentally reshaped Swedish governance. He adopted a new constitution that dramatically expanded royal power, effectively ending 50 years of parliamentary rule known as the "Age of Liberty." Gustav was an enlightened despot — a fan of Voltaire and the arts — which made his power grab more palatable. He'd go on to become one of Sweden's most memorable monarchs, even if his style of rule didn't exactly scream "democracy."

An enlightened despot — which is basically a dictator who reads books and enjoys the opera. Gustav III was a proper Renaissance prince: sorted the economy, built theatres, and made Sweden a cultural capital. He just wasn't too keen on letting anyone else have a say. Enlightened? Yes. Despot? Also yes. Sweden's Age of Liberty ended with a velvet rope and a polite "thank you for your service."

🇹🇷 The Edirne Incident: Sultan Ousted (1703)

August 21, 1703

The Edirne Incident saw the Turkish army rise up against Sultan Mustafa II, forcing his abdication and replacing him with his brother, Ahmed III. The rebellion was driven by anger over the Treaty of Karlowitz (which had ceded massive Ottoman territories) and the Sultan's preference for living in Edirne over Constantinople. The Janissaries — the elite military corps — marched on the capital and made their demands clear. Mustafa II stepped down, and the Ottoman Empire got itself a new sultan without the usual bloodbath.

The army decided the Sultan wasn't cutting it and gave him the boot. No civil war, no execution — just "pack your things, Mustafa, your brother's taking over." The Edirne Incident proved that even the Ottoman throne wasn't safe when the military got grumpy. A lesson in keeping your generals happy and showing up to work in the right city.

🧮 Burroughs's Adding Machine Patent (1888)

August 21, 1888

American inventor William Seward Burroughs — grandfather to the Beat Generation writer William S. Burroughs — patented the first practical adding machine on this day. His invention was a proper game-changer for businesses drowning in paperwork: a machine that could add long columns of numbers reliably. It wasn't the first mechanical calculator, but it was the first that actually worked well enough for daily commercial use. The company he founded eventually became the Burroughs Corporation, a giant in early computing.

The Victorian-era spreadsheet. Before Burroughs, every merchant had a quill, a headache, and a hope. After Burroughs, numbers stopped lying. And the family name didn't stop with calculators — his grandson wrote Naked Lunch. One Burroughs built the machine that counted the money; the other took the money and wrote about mixing heroin, typewriters, and paranoia. What a family tree.

🏏 Botham's Record Wicket at The Oval (1986)

August 21, 1986

England cricket legend Ian Botham took his world-record 356th Test wicket against New Zealand at The Oval, surpassing Dennis Lillee's previous record. Botham — already famous for his heroics with the bat in the 1981 Ashes — was a proper all-rounder in the truest sense. He bowled fast, batted hard, and lived even harder. The record stood until 1994, when Kapil Dev surpassed it, but in 1986, Botham was simply the greatest wicket-taker Test cricket had ever seen.

Beefy Botham — larger than life, hungrier than a teenager after cricket practice. The man could bowl, bat, catch, and probably drink you under the table before lunch. His 356th wicket was a coronation: the greatest all-rounder of his generation breaking a record few thought would fall. Pure 80s England energy — floppy hair, sweatbands, and making the Kiwis look ordinary.

🎵 BTS Drop "Dynamite" — and Break the Internet (2020)

August 21, 2020

Korean pop group BTS released their all-English single "Dynamite" — a funky, feel-good disco-pop banger that became the first video to rack up over 100 million views on YouTube within 24 hours. In a world still reeling from COVID-19 lockdowns, "Dynamite" was a shot of pure joy. It hit number one on the Billboard Hot 100, making BTS the first all-Korean act to top the chart. The song became a global phenomenon, proving that language barriers mean nothing when the beat is right.

Seven Korean lads, a disco beat, and a chorus so catchy it should come with a warning label. "Dynamite" wasn't just a song — it was a cultural reset. 100 million views in a day. The ARMY army is real, and they don't mess about. The pandemic needed a pick-me-up, and BTS delivered. Dynamite indeed.

🎸 Nickelback's "How You Remind Me" Drops (2001)

August 21, 2001

Canadian rock band Nickelback released "How You Remind Me" — the single that would go on to become Billboard's Song of the Year in 2002 and one of the most polarising hits in modern rock history. Love 'em or hate 'em, the song was inescapable: it topped charts worldwide, defined the post-grunge sound of the early 2000s, and became the anthem for everyone who'd ever been "so much older than they felt." Whether you cranked it up or changed the station, you remember exactly where you were when you first heard it.

Look, every generation has its guilty pleasure. Nickelback became the internet's favourite punching bag, but "How You Remind Me" is an absolute banger and you know it. The riff. The gravel. The "this is how you remind me." It was 2001 — flip phones, frosted tips, and Chad Kroeger's voice on every radio in North America. You can run from it, but you can't hide. This is how you remind me of 2001. 😏
