🔥 The Valais Witch Trials Begin (1428)

August 7, 1428

The very first organised witch trials in history kicked off in the Valais Canton of Switzerland. Over the following years, hundreds of people — mostly women, but plenty of blokes too — were accused of consorting with the devil, cursing crops, and generally being a bit too mysterious for the local clergy's liking. This was the trial run, as it were, for the mass hysteria that would sweep across Europe for the next three centuries.

The template for paranoia that would burn tens of thousands across Europe. Started here, in a quiet Swiss valley, because someone's cow got sick and an old woman looked at it funny. Charming start to the genre.

🏯 General Cao Qin Stages a Ming Dynasty Coup (1461)

August 7, 1461

Chinese military general Cao Qin launched a coup against the Tianshun Emperor of the Ming Dynasty. Cao and his co-conspirators set fire to the eastern and western gates of the Forbidden City in a bid to seize power. Spoiler: it didn't work. The uprising was crushed, and Cao was executed. The Ming Dynasty rolled on for another 83 years.

Setting fire to your own emperor's palace gates — bold strategy, Cotton. Let's see if it pays off. (It did not pay off.)

⚓ Russia Gets Its Sea Legs at Gangut (1714)

August 7, 1714

The Battle of Gangut saw the fledgling Russian Navy claim its first major victory, smashing a Swedish fleet during the Great Northern War. Peter the Great's obsession with building a navy finally paid off — Russia proved it could fight on water as well as land, much to Sweden's dismay. This victory essentially kicked the door open for Russia to become a Baltic naval power.

Peter the Great built a navy from scratch because he fancied having one. Most people buy a rowing boat. This bloke built an empire's worth of warships. Different energy entirely.

🎖️ The Birth of the Purple Heart (1782)

August 7, 1782

General George Washington, Commander of the Continental Army, created the Badge of Military Merit — the first time in history that military awards were presented to common soldiers rather than just officers. Two Honorary Badges of Distinction were also created. The Badge of Military Merit later became the Purple Heart, one of America's most recognisable military decorations.

Washington figured the blokes doing the actual fighting deserved a bit of recognition too. Revolutionary in more ways than one, eh?

📻 The Transistor Radio Revolution Begins (1955)

August 7, 1955

Tokyo Telecommunications Engineering — a company you might know better as Sony — began selling its first transistor radios in Japan. These pocket-sized devices changed everything: music went from a living-room experience to something you could take on the bus. The first model was the TR-55, and it kicked off the portable electronics revolution. Sony never looked back.

Before this, "portable music" meant hiring a bloke with a tuba to follow you around. Sony made the world pocket-sized. Proper bit of genius.

🪖 Desert Shield Goes Up (1990)

August 7, 1990

The United States deployed troops to Saudi Arabia, kicking off Operation Desert Shield — the defensive phase of the Gulf War. Just five days earlier, Saddam Hussein's Iraq had invaded Kuwait. The deployment was the largest US military build-up in the Middle East since... well, ever at that point. It would lead to Operation Desert Storm the following January.

The one where the world said "right, that's enough" and sent half a million troops to tell Saddam to pack it in. Proper show of force.

💔 The Simele Massacre (1933)

August 7, 1933

Iraqi government forces slaughtered over 3,000 Assyrians in the village of Simele in northern Iraq, in what is now commemorated as Assyrian Martyrs Day. The massacre was part of a wider campaign of violence against the Assyrian Christian community, who had sought refuge in Iraq after the collapse of the Ottoman Empire. A dark chapter that still resonates today.

A brutal reminder that sectarian violence in the region didn't start yesterday. Three thousand souls, wiped out because of who they were and what they believed. Buried but not forgotten.

🥎 Babe Ruth Crushes It — Twice (1929)

August 7, 1929

Babe Ruth, the New York Yankees' slugger extraordinaire, tied an MLB record by hitting grand slams in consecutive games for the second time in his career. The Yankees thumped the Philadelphia Athletics 13-1. Ruth was already a legend, but hitting grand slams in back-to-back games — twice — is the kind of flex that keeps you in the history books a century later.

Babe Ruth hit grand slams in consecutive games not once, but twice. The man basically invented showing off. Absolute menace at the plate.

🎵 Tik Tok, Time's Up (2009)

August 7, 2009

Kesha released her debut single "Tik Tok" — a song that would go on to become the Billboard Song of the Year 2010 and a cultural phenomenon. Love it or hate it, you absolutely remember brushing your teeth with a bottle of Jack. The track spent nine weeks at #1 in the US and became one of the best-selling digital singles of all time.

Say what you will about Kesha, but that song was absolutely everywhere. You couldn't escape it. Not that most of us wanted to, mind. Don't stop, make it pop, etc.

🐻 Winnie the Pooh — Banned in China (2018)

August 7, 2018

China banned the release of the Winnie the Pooh movie "Christopher Robin" after the beloved honey-loving bear became a popular meme used to mock Chinese President Xi Jinping. The resemblance between Xi and Pooh had been circulating online for years, and the Chinese government — never one for a laugh at the leader's expense — decided the best response was to ban a children's film. Absolutely no sense of humour, that lot.

Imagine being so thin-skinned that a fictional bear who talks to owls and wears his trousers too high becomes a threat to national security. You couldn't write it — though, er, someone did. A.A. Milne, 1926.

💍 Wedding Bells: Zeppelins, Flintstones, and the Man in Black

August 7 — Love Through the Ages

1869: German airship inventor Ferdinand von Zeppelin (31) wed Isabella Freiin von Wolff in Berlin. The man who'd go on to fill the skies with giant floating gas bags tied the knot — hopefully with more stability than his early airships.

1936: American animator William Hanna (26) married Violet Blanch Wogatzke (23). The future co-creator of The Flintstones and Tom & Jerry started his own domestic partnership — no dinosaurs or cartoon mice involved.

1954: Country music legend Johnny Cash (22) married his first wife Vivian Liberto (20) in San Antonio, Texas. The marriage lasted until 1966 — not bad for a man who sang about walking the line.

Three very different blokes tying the knot. One built airships, one drew cartoons, and one wore black and sang about prisons. What a day for matrimony.
