Friday, 31 July 2026

⛪ Pilgrim Fathers Set Sail for Freedom (1620)

July 31, 1620

The Pilgrim Fathers departed Leiden, Netherlands, heading for England — and eventually, America. A group of religious separatists who'd fled English persecution for the Netherlands, they decided the New World looked a bit more promising. Their journey would end with the Mayflower and a rather famous feast involving turkeys and questionable hats.

Left one country for religious freedom, went to another, then decided to cross an ocean for more. Dedication, that. Or just really picky about neighbours.

💥 Passchendaele — Mud, Blood, and Pointlessness (1917)

July 31, 1917

The Battle of Passchendaele (Third Battle of Ypres) kicked off in Belgium, dragging on until November 6, 1917. Approximately 500,000 casualties were suffered for a few miles of mud-soaked ground. The name alone has become shorthand for the sheer futility of trench warfare — men drowning in shell holes and sinking into the muck.

Half a million souls for a patch of mud you wouldn't let your dog sleep on. War at its most senseless. Properly grim stuff.

🚂 America's First Railway Track — Made of Wood (1809)

July 31, 1809

The first practical US railroad track was laid in Philadelphia, though it was made of wood and designed for horse-drawn cars. Not exactly the Iron Horse just yet — more of a splintery pony. Still, somebody had to start somewhere, and wood was cheap.

A wooden track for horse carts. The American rail empire started with a glorified sled path. Gotta start somewhere, I suppose.

📽️ No More Punching for the Cameras (1912)

July 31, 1912

The US government banned the filming and interstate distribution of boxing prizefight films. They were worried about the moral decay of the nation — or perhaps just annoyed that people were more interested in watching blokes wallop each other than reading newspapers. The ban stood until 1940.

Government censors decided you couldn't watch people punch each other on film for 28 years. Meanwhile, the fights kept happening. Well, that fixed everything, didn't it?

🥏 Halina Konopacka Flings Her Way Into History (1928)

July 31, 1928

Polish athlete Halina Konopacka hurled a discus world record of 39.62 metres at the Amsterdam Olympics, winning the first-ever gold medal in women's Olympic athletics. A proper bit of sporting history — and a throw that would still embarrass most blokes in the pub garden.

First woman to win Olympic athletics gold, and she did it with a world record. Absolute queen of the discus. No notes.

🎸 Cliff Richard Goes to #1 with Living Doll (1959)

July 31, 1959

Cliff Richard and the Drifters scored their first British #1 single with "Living Doll", which became the best-selling British single of 1959. Cliff would go on to have a career spanning decades, multiple Christmas #1s, and an uncanny ability to never age. Some suspect he's actually a waxwork.

Living Doll was a cracker, but let's be honest — Cliff's been peddling the same cheeky-chap charm for 60-odd years. Fair play to the bloke, but it's a bit spooky at this point.

🇮🇱 Israel Hits the Million Mark (1961)

July 31, 1961

Israel welcomed its one-millionth immigrant, a significant milestone for a nation founded just 13 years earlier. In the decades since, the population has swelled to over 9 million — a testament to the country's role as a homeland for Jewish people worldwide.

🍹 Black Tot Day — The Navy Gets a Sobriety Test (1970)

July 31, 1970

Black Tot Day marked the end of the Royal Navy's official rum ration, a tradition dating back to 1740. For 230 years, British sailors got a daily tot of rum — until the Admiralty decided modern warships and tipsy ratings weren't the best combo. Sailors marked the occasion with mock funerals for their beloved grog. Emotional stuff.

Two hundred and thirty years of daily booze on the high seas, killed off by health and safety. The Navy was essentially a floating pub with guns for two centuries. God bless 'em.

🤝 Operation Banner Finally Flies Home (2007)

July 31, 2007

Operation Banner — the British Army's presence in Northern Ireland — officially came to an end after 38 years. It was the longest continuous operation in British military history, beginning during the Troubles and ending as peace took hold. A proper milestone for Northern Ireland, and proof that even the longest conflicts can find resolution.

38 years is a hell of a shift. From the darkest days of the Troubles to a functioning peace — Northern Ireland's come a long way. Fair play to everyone who made that happen.

💍 Wedding Bells & Broken Ties

July 31 — Love and Loss Through the Ages

1790: Danish Crown Prince Frederick (later Frederick VI) married his cousin Marie Sophie of Hesse-Kassel. Royalty keeping it in the family, as per tradition.

1940: American actress Loretta Young (27) married advertising exec Tom Lewis. Divorced in 1969 — turns out Hollywood marriages don't always have happy endings.

1948: Aspiring screenwriter Rod Serling (23) married college classmate Carol Kramer (19). He'd go on to create The Twilight Zone — arguably the most famous twist endings in TV history.

1980: Karolyn Englehardt divorced baseball legend Pete Rose after 16 years. Not the only controversy in Rose's life, but certainly a personal one.

2024: Rapper Cardi B filed for divorce from Offset for the second time. Some couples just can't stay together — or apart.

Rod Serling getting married on this day feels appropriate — his whole career was about things not being what they seem. Bit like marriage, really.
