Friday, July 17, 2026

180 AD

Carthage Christians Make the Ultimate Sacrifice

Six residents of Carthage, North Africa, are executed for their Christian faith — the earliest recorded instance of Christianity in the region. The Roman Empire wasn't exactly handing out medals for religious diversity back then. These six blokes were martyred for their beliefs, and their names are lost to history, but their courage marks the beginning of a faith that would eventually transform the entire continent. Not bad for a handful of believers in what's now Tunisia.

1203

Crusaders Go Rogue in Constantinople

The Siege of Constantinople kicks off during the Fourth Crusade, as Roman Catholic Crusaders aboard a Venetian fleet attack the Byzantine capital. This was meant to be a crusade to reclaim Jerusalem, but it went completely off the rails — the Venetians redirected the whole thing to settle a trade dispute. The Crusaders sacked the city in 1204, carving up the Byzantine Empire like a Sunday roast. The whole debacle left a bitter taste in Eastern Orthodox mouths that lingers to this day. Proper dirty business.

1717

Handel's Water Music Makes a Splash

George Frideric Handel's "Water Music" premieres — and not in some stuffy concert hall, but on a barge cruising down the River Thames in London. King George I was so chuffed with the performance that he had the musicians play the whole suite three times, floating up and down the river. The king was clearly a man who knew how to party. The music is still an absolute banger three centuries later — proof that a good tune never goes out of style.

1762

Catherine the Great Takes the Throne

Empress Catherine II becomes Tsarina of Russia after the death — and rumoured assassination — of her husband, Tsar Peter III. Catherine was German-born, clever as a whip, and not about to let a useless husband stand in her way. She went on to rule for 34 years, modernising Russia, expanding its borders, and collecting art, lovers, and enemies with equal enthusiasm. Whether Peter's death was natural or a little nudge from the palace guard remains one of history's great unanswered questions. Either way, Russia got the better end of the deal.

1816

The Raft of the Medusa — A Grisly Tale

The French ship "L'Argus" accidentally discovers a raft holding survivors from the wrecked French frigate Méduse. After 13 days adrift at sea, only 15 of the original 151 souls remain. The rest? Cannibalised, murdered, or driven to suicide by the sheer horror of their situation. The French government tried to cover the whole thing up, but artist Théodore Géricault immortalised the tragedy in his massive painting "The Raft of the Medusa" — a proper masterpiece that captures the desperation, madness, and sheer will to survive. Grim stuff, mate.

1959

North by Northwest: Hitchcock's Masterpiece Hits the Big Screen

Alfred Hitchcock's "North by Northwest" premieres in Los Angeles, starring Cary Grant, Eva Marie Saint, and a crop-duster that's terrified audiences ever since. The film is a perfect blend of spy thriller, comedy, and near-constant tension — with Grant's Roger Thornhill being chased across America by both foreign agents and the police. The Mount Rushmore finale is iconic, and the "I'm a big boy" train scene still sets hearts racing. A proper bit of cinematic genius from the Master of Suspense.

1976

Montreal Olympics Open — With a Boycott

The 21st modern Olympic Games open in Montreal, but 25 African teams (eventually rising to 33 nations) boycott the event. The reason? New Zealand's rugby team had toured apartheid South Africa, and the African nations demanded the International Olympic Committee ban New Zealand in protest. The IOC refused, so Africa walked. The games went ahead but were a financial disaster for Montreal — the city was paying off Olympic debt for decades. Bleedin' politics always gets in the way of sport, doesn't it?

2025

Colbert's Late Show Gets the Chop

Exactly one year ago today, CBS announces the cancellation of "The Late Show With Stephen Colbert" after a decade-long run since 2015. Colbert, who took over the legendary Ed Sullivan Theatre after David Letterman retired, had been a steady voice of satire through some of America's most chaotic years. The show signed off with its signature mix of sharp political commentary, absurdist sketches, and the host's increasingly unhinged monologues. The era of late-night TV as we knew it took another hit.

💡 Did You Know?

Also on July 17, 1918 — The Russian Imperial Romanov family (Tsar Nicholas II, his wife Alexandra, and their five children) were executed by Bolshevik revolutionaries in a cellar in Yekaterinburg. Their bodies were buried in a secret grave and weren't discovered until 1979. The Russian Orthodox Church later canonised them as martyrs. A grim end to a dynasty that had ruled for over 300 years.

And in 1997 — The F.W. Woolworth Company, the original five-and-dime retail chain that had been a fixture on every Main Street in America, closed its final remaining stores after 117 years in business. The end of an era for bargain shopping.
