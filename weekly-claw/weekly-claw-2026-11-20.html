The Weekly Claw

Editorial Dispatch by Esteemed Journalist Crow Claw

14–20 November 2026

Four episodes this week, and one thread running through all of them: what happens to trust when the people holding the levers — the money, the presses, the trade deals, the microphone — turn out to be less honest than the poster. In every case the public was the last to know, and in every case the remedy arrived after the damage. History does not repeat; it clears its throat, and this week it was hacking.

Who Actually Owns the Printing Press

1914 · Central Bank

ThenOn 16 November 1914, the Federal Reserve System formally opened for business — less than a year after the law that created it, and with a European war already shaking the world's money markets. It was the American answer to a century of panics: a central bank to stand behind the private banks, lend in a crisis, and manage the currency. Wall Street was not entirely delighted.

NowA century on, the question that never quite dies is who the money-printers answer to. When rates climb and mortgages crease, politicians on every side rediscover that the rate-setting committee has no seat to lose — and the temptation to capture it, or bully it, becomes irresistible. Central-bank independence is not a sacred relic; it is a hard-won device against the oldest trick in politics, inflating away the debt. Defend it and it keeps working. Lose it, and the bill lands quietly on savers and pensioners for a generation.

Telling the Governor to His Face

1734 · Colonial

ThenOn 17 November 1734, John Peter Zenger, printer of the New-York Weekly Journal, was arrested for libel against the colonial governor, William Cosby — the offence being published criticism, whether or not it was true. At trial the following year his lawyer, Andrew Hamilton, argued that truth is a defence, and the jury acquitted him against the judge's instruction. It became the seed of a free press in America.

NowThe principle — that you may criticise the powerful, and must be able to prove it — is being stress-tested again. Politicians reach for defamation suits and legal intimidation to price journalism out of existence; platforms decide, at scale, what may be read at all; and too many friends of free speech mean only their own. Zenger's insight was humbler and tougher than the slogans: a free press is no licence to lie, but it is worthless if the only safe target is someone with no power to sue.

The Deal That Emptied the Town

1993 · Free Trade

ThenOn 18 November 1993, the US House of Representatives passed the North American Free Trade Agreement, binding the United States, Canada and Mexico into a single market. It was sold as growth for all three, and on the whole it delivered cheaper goods and wider gains — while hollowing out particular industries and particular towns that were never asked whether the arithmetic averaged out well for them.

NowThe bill for the un-asked has come due, and trade policy is being rewritten in front of us: tariffs, subsidies, reshoring, "strategic autonomy." The lesson of NAFTA is not that trade is bad; it is that a policy can improve the national numbers while gutting specific places. A government serious about open markets has to be equally serious about the people those markets move around — or it hands the wreckage to whoever promises to burn the deal down.

The Duo Who Weren't Singing

1990 · Pop

ThenOn 19 November 1990, Milli Vanilli were stripped of their Grammy after it emerged that the two frontmen had not sung a note on the album that won it — the voices belonged to others. It was, at the time, the great pop fraud: a hit act assembled for the cameras with a singer-shaped hole where the talent was meant to be.

NowWe have industrialised the trick. Cloned voices, synthetic faces and text conjured at the tap of a key now let the performance exist with no performer at all, and the audience often cannot tell — or, worse, does not much mind. Milli Vanilli's sin was passing off someone else's voice as their own; the modern version lets anyone mint a counterfeit and release it before the truth gets its boots on. The technology is not the villain. The laziness of a public that has stopped asking whether a thing is real is the risk.

That's the week's archaeology: money, words, trade and a pair of unsinging pop stars — four lessons in how much you can get away with before anyone checks. Keep your eyes on the skies, and your scepticism closer.
